1. Surface / Open Wells
(Persian Wheel, Lever/Dhenkli, Mhote)		 • High water table (within 10–15 m depth).
• Soft alluvial soil (easy to dig).
• Clayey bed below to hold water.		 • Very cheap & simple to dig for poor farmers.
• Independent source (used whenever needed).
• Oxen kept for ploughing can operate Persian wheel at zero fuel cost.		 • Irrigates a very small area (1 to 2 hectares only).
• Dries up during summer when water table drops.
• Well water may be brackish (salty).		 Uttar Pradesh (#1), Bihar, Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, Gujarat.
2. Tubewells
(Deep Bore + Electric/Diesel Pump)		 • Abundant underground water (aquifer).
• Soft, deep alluvial soil.
• Cheap & regular supply of electricity.
• Fertile productive land to recover cost.		 • Irrigates a much larger area (~400+ hectares) than surface wells.
• Perennial/Reliable even during summer droughts (draws from deep aquifers).
• Protected from evaporation & surface dust/pollution.		 • Expensive to bore and install pump sets.
• Requires continuous electricity or diesel (useless during power cuts).
• Over-drafting causes alarming depletion of water table.		 Uttar Pradesh (#1), Punjab, Haryana, Bihar, Gujarat, West Bengal.
3. Inundation Canals
(Flood-Water Canals)		 • Low-lying flat floodplains adjacent to rivers that swell during monsoons. • Very cheap to construct (no expensive dam/barrage needed).
• Helps control monsoon river floods.
• Floodwater brings rich fertile silt.		 • Seasonal / Non-perennial — flows only during rainy floods.
• Useless during dry summer & winter (Rabi) when crops need water most.
• Only irrigates low-lying land.		 Punjab (Sutlej basin), West Bengal, Assam (mostly converted to perennial now).
4. Perennial Canals
(Dam/Barrage + Regulator)		 • Perennial snow-fed rivers.
• Flat, gently sloping plain.
• Soft soil for easy canal digging.		 • Provides water throughout the year (both Kharif & Rabi crops).
• Transformed arid Rajasthan (Indira Gandhi Canal) & Punjab into granaries.
• Cheap per hectare once built.		 • Seepage from unlined canals causes Waterlogging.
• Capillary action brings up alkaline salts (‘Reh’ or ‘Kallar’), making soil barren.
• High initial construction cost & breeding ground for mosquitoes.		 Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, Bihar, Andhra Pradesh.
5. Tank Irrigation
(Earthen/Masonry Bund across Depression)		 • Hard, non-porous (impervious) crystalline rock underneath (prevents seepage).
• Uneven, undulating rocky plateau with natural depressions.		 • Stores monsoon runoff that would otherwise flow wasted into the sea.
• Natural depressions in Deccan make construction cheap.
• Only viable method where hard rock prevents well/canal digging.		 • Non-perennial: Tanks dry up in hot summer when needed most.
• Heavy loss of water by evaporation due to large shallow surface area.
• Silting of tank beds requires frequent desilting & occupies large fertile area.		 Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Maharashtra.
6. Drip Irrigation
(Perforated Pipes at Plant Roots)		 • Arid/semi-arid regions & high-value orchards, vegetables, plantation crops. • Highest water efficiency (saves 40–70% water).
• Zero loss by evaporation or runoff (delivers water drop-by-drop to roots).
• Prevents weed growth & allows fertigation (liquid fertilizer).		 • High initial capital cost of pipes/emitters.
• Tiny nozzles/tubes get clogged easily by sediment or salt.
• Pipes can be damaged by rodents or sunlight.		 Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Gujarat, Rajasthan, Andhra Pradesh.
7. Sprinkler Irrigation
(Overhead Revolving Nozzles)		 • Uneven, undulating, or sandy/hilly terrain where levelling is impossible. • No loss of water by seepage or open channel evaporation.
• Does not require land levelling (ideal for slopes/sandy soil).
• Uniform distribution like natural rain.		 • Expensive to purchase and operate.
• High winds deflect water spray & cause evaporation loss.
• High humidity on leaves can cause fungal crop diseases; only for small areas.		 Haryana, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala (Tea/Coffee estates).
Traditional RWH Name Region / State in India Nature & Mechanism (Official Board Keywords)
1. Johads & Khadins Rajasthan (Central & Western — Jaisalmer) Crescent-shaped earthen check dams / embankments built across seasonal streams to capture rainwater runoff and percolate it to recharge groundwater.
2. Tankas / Kunds Western Rajasthan (Thar Desert — Bikaner, Barmer) & Gujarat Covered underground cylindrical storage tanks (lined with lime) built inside house courtyards to store rooftop rainwater for drinking during dry summers.
3. Khatri & Kuhl Western Himalayas (Himachal Pradesh & Jammu) Kuhl: Surface diversion channels carrying glacial/stream water to terraced fields.
Khatri: Chambers carved inside hard mountain rock where rainwater seeps through rock fissures and is stored.
4. Zing Ladakh (Cold Desert) Small tanks/reservoirs that collect melted glacier water through a network of channels during the day for irrigation.
5. Surangam & Korambu Western Ghats (Kerala & Karnataka — Kasaragod) Surangam: Horizontal man-made tunnel dug into a laterite hill slope from which groundwater seeps out.
Korambu: Temporary dam of brushwood, mud, and grass built across channels in Kerala.
6. Cheruvu & Eris Andhra Pradesh (Cheruvu) & Tamil Nadu (Eris / Ooranis) Large community storage reservoirs/tanks built to store monsoon surface runoff for irrigation and recharging surrounding wells.
7. Bhandaras & Kere Deccan Plateau (Maharashtra — Bhandaras; Karnataka — Kere) Check dams / diversion weirs built across rivers to raise water level and channel it into canals or storage tanks.
8. Dongs / Dungs & Zabo Assam (Brahmaputra Valley — Bodo tribes) & Nagaland (Zabo) Dongs: Ponds constructed by Bodo farmers in Assam to harvest rainwater for paddy irrigation.
9. Baolis / Dighis / Jhalaras Gangetic Plains (Delhi, UP — Baolis/Dighis) & Rajasthan/Gujarat (Stepwells) Traditional masonry stepwells with descending flights of steps to reach the fluctuating groundwater table.
10. Bamboo Drip System Meghalaya (Khasi & Jaintia Hills) 200-year-old ingenious system using split bamboo pipes to divert hill-stream water over hundreds of metres by gravity, reducing flow to 20–80 drops/minute at betel leaf & black pepper roots.
🏠 4 Mandatory Components of a Modern Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting System (For Diagram Questions):
1. Catchment: The terrace or rooftop of the building that receives rainfall directly.
2. Conduits (Downpipes & Gutters): PVC or GI drainpipes that carry rainwater from the rooftop catchment down to the ground.
3. First-Flush Valve & Filter Unit: Diverts the dirty first shower (containing bird droppings/dust) and filters the clean water through sand, gravel, charcoal, and boulders.
4. Recharge Facility / Storage Tank: Underground storage tank or Percolation / Recharge Pit, Trench, or Abandoned Dugwell/Borewell that recharges the underground aquifer.
